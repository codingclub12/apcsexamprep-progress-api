💡 Think about this first

A museum has ten thousand old handwritten weather logs it wants turned into a searchable database, but its staff of three would need years to type them all in. Instead it posts scans online and asks the public to transcribe a page or two each. Within weeks the whole archive is done by strangers who never met. What made that possible, and what is this approach actually called?

What Crowdsourcing Is

Crowdsourcing is the practice of obtaining input, ideas, content, labor, or funding from a large group of people, often through the Internet, to accomplish a task or solve a problem. The key word is large. Instead of a small, fixed team doing all the work, a task is opened up to a big, often open crowd, and each contributor supplies a small piece. Add those pieces together and you get a result no small group could have produced as quickly, if at all.

The crowd can provide different things depending on the project. Sometimes it provides labor (people transcribing documents or classifying images), sometimes data (volunteers reporting bird sightings or road conditions), sometimes ideas or content (writing entries, submitting designs), and sometimes money (many small donations funding a project). Naming exactly what the crowd contributes is a favorite exam move.

🎯 What the exam rewards

When a scenario is crowdsourcing, the exam wants you to point to two things: a large group of people each contributing a small part, and the fact that the Internet or computing is what makes gathering those contributions at scale possible. If you can name both, you can answer almost any 5.4 question.

Why Computing Makes It Work

Crowdsourcing is an old idea, but the Internet transformed it. Computing gives a crowdsourced project three things a small team cannot match on its own:

  • Reach. A request posted online can be seen by millions of people across the world, so the potential pool of contributors is enormous.
  • Speed. Thousands of people can work in parallel at the same moment, so a task that would take one team years can finish in days.
  • Scale. Software collects, combines, and stores every small contribution automatically, so the size of the crowd is not limited by how much a coordinator can manually track.

Take those away and crowdsourcing shrinks back to whoever you can reach in person. That is why the framework ties crowdsourcing so tightly to computing: the Internet is not a minor detail of these examples, it is the enabling technology that gives the crowd its reach, speed, and scale.

Quick check
A wildlife group asks people everywhere to photograph frogs in their area and upload the pictures with a location, building a nationwide map of where each species lives. What is the crowd primarily providing?

The Main Kinds of Crowdsourcing

The exam expects you to recognize a few recurring flavors of crowdsourcing by their example, not by memorizing a definition word for word:

Type What the crowd provides Example
Citizen science Data and labor: ordinary volunteers collect or classify observations People worldwide count backyard birds or tag galaxies in telescope images so scientists can study far more data than a lab could gather alone.
Crowdfunding Money: many small contributions add up to fund a project An inventor raises money for a new gadget by collecting small pledges from thousands of online backers instead of one big investor.
Collaborative content Ideas and content: the public writes, edits, or reviews shared material A public reference site where volunteers write and correct entries, or product ratings where many buyers each leave a review.

Notice they differ only in what the crowd supplies (data, money, or content) while sharing the same core: a large, open group each doing a little, gathered through the Internet.

⚠ Common trap

Crowdsourcing is not just any group working together. A five-person class project or a company's normal design team is collaboration, but it is not crowdsourcing, because the group is small and closed. Crowdsourcing draws on a large, often open group, usually reached through the Internet. If the scenario has a small fixed team, it is collaboration, not crowdsourcing.

Benefits and Concerns

The upside of crowdsourcing is harnessing the knowledge, effort, or money of many people to do something no small team could. A crowd can label millions of images, spot a rare event happening somewhere in the world, or fund a project that no single backer would risk on.

But scale brings problems the exam also expects you to know. Quality and accuracy vary because contributions come from strangers with mixed expertise, so crowdsourced data or content usually needs verification. Coordination is hard when thousands of people work loosely on the same task. And attribution gets murky: when a crowd builds something together, deciding who gets credit or ownership is not obvious. Good crowdsourcing platforms design for these problems with review steps, guidelines, and reputation systems.

Quick check
A startup's five employees meet each week to design their app together. A rival instead posts its design online and invites thousands of users worldwide to submit and vote on ideas. Which situation is crowdsourcing, and why?

How 5.4 Is Tested on the Multiple-Choice Exam

Crowdsourcing shows up in the multiple-choice section as short scenario questions, and there is no free-response component specific to this topic. Two skills earn the points. First, recognizing crowdsourcing: given a scenario, decide whether it counts, and the test is whether a large, often open group is each contributing a small piece, as opposed to a small fixed team (which is ordinary collaboration). Second, identifying what makes it possible: questions frequently ask why the approach works, and the intended answer is that the Internet and computing provide the reach, speed, and scale to gather many contributions that a small team could not. Expect distractors that describe small-group collaboration, or that credit better hardware or faster coding instead of the crowd and the network. Know the flavors (citizen science, crowdfunding, collaborative content) so you can name what the crowd supplies, and you will clear these quickly.

Which of the following scenarios is the best example of crowdsourcing?

Incorrect. Five engineers at one company is a small, closed team. That is collaboration, not crowdsourcing.
Correct. A large, open group of drivers each contributes a small report, and the Internet gathers those contributions at scale into a shared result. That is crowdsourcing.
Incorrect. One scientist working alone is the opposite of a crowd. Crowdsourcing needs input from a large group.

Question 2 of 6Role of computing
Name what the Internet contributes before you look.

A charity used to raise money by mailing letters to a few hundred local residents. It now posts its campaign online, where anyone in the world can donate. Which best explains how computing changed what the charity can do?

Incorrect. Crowdsourcing does not guarantee larger individual donations. Its power is the number of contributors, not the size of each gift.
Incorrect. A crowdfunding campaign still needs a clear goal. Computing changes the reach, not the need for a plan.
Incorrect. Accuracy of arithmetic is not the issue. The change is scale and reach, letting the crowd grow far beyond a local mailing list.
Correct. The Internet gives the campaign reach and scale, letting it collect many small contributions from far more people than local mail ever could. That enabling role is the point of 5.4.

Incorrect. Pooling money among four friends is not crowdfunding. The defining feature of crowdsourcing is a large, open group, not just more than one contributor.
Correct. Four friends are a small, closed group, so this is ordinary collaboration. Crowdsourcing requires a large, often open crowd, typically reached through the Internet.
Incorrect. Reaching a decision together is collaboration. Crowdsourcing specifically draws on a large, often open group, usually via the Internet.
Incorrect. Crowdsourcing can involve money (crowdfunding). The real reason this is not crowdsourcing is that the group is small and closed.
Question 4 of 6II and III style

Consider these statements about crowdsourcing:

  • I. Crowdsourcing can only be used to raise money, never to gather data or labor.
  • II. The Internet gives crowdsourcing reach, speed, and scale that a small team could not achieve alone.
  • III. Because contributions come from a large, varied group of strangers, quality and accuracy can be a concern.
Correct. II and III are both accurate: the Internet enables scale, and quality of mixed contributions is a real concern. I is false because crowdsourcing is not limited to money.
Incorrect. Statement I is false: crowdsourcing gathers data and labor too, not only money. So it cannot be part of the answer.
Incorrect. Statement I is false, so any choice that includes I is wrong, even though III is true.
Incorrect. Statement I is false, so all three cannot be correct.
Question 5 of 6Identify the type
Predict which flavor of crowdsourcing this is first.

An astronomy project posts millions of telescope images online and asks volunteers around the world to classify the shape of each galaxy, producing labeled data that researchers analyze. Which term best describes this activity?

Correct. Volunteers collecting or classifying scientific data at scale is citizen science, a form of crowdsourcing where the crowd supplies data and labor.
Incorrect. No money is being raised. Crowdfunding is crowdsourcing money, and this scenario gathers classifications, not funds.
Incorrect. Pair programming is two people sharing one computer to write code. It has nothing to do with a global crowd labeling images.
Incorrect. A single research team dividing work is small and closed. Here the labeling is done by a large, open crowd of volunteers online.
Question 6 of 6Concern
Predict the most likely downside before reading the options.

A travel site lets anyone post reviews and star ratings of hotels, and it shows the average rating for each hotel. Which of the following is the most significant concern a computer scientist would raise about this crowdsourced data?

Incorrect. Storage capacity is not the crowdsourcing concern here. Computing easily stores large numbers of reviews.
Incorrect. Averaging ratings is straightforward arithmetic. The real concern is the trustworthiness of the individual contributions.
Incorrect. The opposite is true: the Internet is exactly what lets the site reach a large audience. Reach is a benefit, not the concern.
Correct. Because contributions come from a large group of unverified strangers, quality and accuracy vary, and fake or biased reviews are a genuine risk. Verification is the core concern with crowdsourced content.
